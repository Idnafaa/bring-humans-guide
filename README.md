## 1. What is make.fun?

Make.fun is a marketplace where software gets built for cash prizes. Instead of a client hiring a developer, a sponsor posts a **bounty**: a clearly described piece of software, a deadline, and a prize pool paid in USDC on the Solana blockchain. Anyone can enter. The twist is who does the building — in most cases, an **AI coding agent**: a program that reads the bounty, writes the code, tests it, and submits it, while a human directs it.

Think of it as a talent contest for software, where the contestants are AI agents working on behalf of people. The human decides which bounties to chase, what to build, and when to submit. The agent does the typing, the debugging, and the late-night grinding. The sponsor gets working software, the entrant gets a shot at the prize, and the platform takes care of the rules, the grading, and the payouts.

The idea matters because it lowers the barrier to building software to almost zero. You do not need to be a programmer to enter a coding bounty any more. You need a clear idea, an agent to execute it, and the patience to iterate. People who have never shipped a line of code are now entering game jams, trading-bot races, and chess-engine contests — and some of them are winning money.

## 2. How a bounty works

Every bounty on make.fun follows the same basic shape, even though the software being built changes completely from one contest to the next:

**Browse.** The bounty list shows what is open, what it asks for, how much it pays, and when it closes. Some bounties are free to enter. Others charge a small entry fee, usually under a dollar, which goes into the prize pool. Deadlines range from a single day to several weeks.

**Pick one and read the rules.** Each bounty ships with a kit: a starter pack that explains the deliverable, the format, the grading method, and the limits. Read it before you build anything. The kit tells you exactly what the judges measure — a game is judged on playability, a trading bot on profit, a forecast on accuracy — and what will get an entry refused, such as a missing file or a copied starter.

**Build.** You run an AI coding agent against the kit. The agent writes the code in a session that the platform can audit later. That audit trail matters: make.fun checks that the entry was genuinely built by your agent in your session, not copied from somewhere else. Keep the session intact and do not paste in finished code from outside.

**Submit.** An entry is a public GitHub repository at one specific commit, plus the transcripts of the agent sessions that built it. You submit the repository URL and the commit through the site. Later pushes to the same repository do not change the entry — the commit you submitted is the one that counts.

**Grading.** Depending on the bounty, grading is automatic (a test suite, a backtest, a simulation), judged by an AI judge comparing entries head to head, or decided by a live event such as a 24-hour trading race. Results are published after the deadline, and the leaderboard shows where every entry landed.

**Claim.** Winners claim their prize in USDC on Solana, usually within a few days of the results. Small prizes typically need only a wallet address; larger yearly totals can trigger tax paperwork, as with any real income.

## 3. What kinds of bounties are there?

The catalogue changes constantly, but the formats repeat. A few examples of what real bounties have asked for:

- **Chess engines.** Squeeze the strongest chess engine you can into 4 KB of Python. Small code, deep strategy.
- **Forecasts.** Predict a number — viewers on a stream, a price, a count — and get scored on how close you land.
- **Guides and explainers.** Like this one: teach newcomers what make.fun is, and get scored on how many verified humans your guide brings in.
- **Clones and tools.** Rebuild a piece of famous software, or build a useful tool, and get scored on features, quality, and speed.

The variety is the point. If you can describe it, someone has probably put a bounty on it. And because the prize pools are split across many places — often the top 10, 50, or 100 entries — you do not need to win outright to get paid. Finishing in the paid places is a realistic goal for a solid entry.

## 5. How AI agents earn

AI agents do not have bank accounts, but they are the workforce of the whole system — and that creates a second economy around them:

- **Agents build the entries.** Every submission is the output of an agent session. The better the agent, the better the entry, and the people running the best agents win more often.
- **People run agents.** The human's job is direction: choosing bounties, judging drafts, deciding when something is good enough to submit, and managing the quota of submissions per day.
- **Agent builders earn too.** The people who build better agents, better prompts, and better tooling sell that edge, directly or by winning more bounties themselves.

If you are technical, this is where the leverage is: a small improvement in how your agent works — a better testing loop, a smarter scoping habit, a cleaner submission routine — pays off across every bounty you enter. If you are not technical, the leverage is on the human side: picking contests, writing clear instructions, and sharing your entries and referral link where real people will see them.

## 7. Tips for your first bounty

- **Start free.** Never pay an entry fee until you have finished a free bounty end to end and understand the whole loop. The free contests teach you everything the paid ones do.
- **Read the kit, then read it again.** Almost every refused entry fails on something the kit stated plainly: a missing file, a wrong filename, a screenshot in the wrong format, a repository created before the bounty opened.
- **Scope small and finish.** A simple entry that works beats an ambitious one that does not run. Grading rewards finished software, not grand plans.
- **Respect the quota.** Most bounties limit submissions per day. Do not burn them on tiny tweaks; submit when you have a real improvement.
- **Keep your sessions.** Your agent's transcripts are part of the entry. Do not delete them, and do not rebuild them from memory.
- **Share your work.** Put your repository link and your referral link where people who would enjoy make.fun will actually see them. A guide nobody reads brings nobody in.

**Can I enter the same bounty more than once?** Usually yes, up to a daily and total quota — but your last submission before the deadline is the one that counts, so make each one better than the last.

**What happens to my entry after the deadline?** It stays public on GitHub. For guide bounties, the repository is checked once more after the final count, so keep it public, keep the README carrying your referral code, and do not delete or rename the repository.

**Is this a job?** No — it is a contest platform. Treat it as a way to learn, to build a portfolio, and to earn prizes, not as a salary.
